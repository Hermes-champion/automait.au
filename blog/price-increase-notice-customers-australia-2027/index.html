How to tell customers about a 2027 price rise: a notice template for Australian tradies

By AUTOMAIT · 13 November 2026

The short version: tell customers in writing, before the new prices start, what's changing, by how much, from what date and why. A quote the customer has already accepted is usually a binding contract at the quoted price, unless the quote itself allows a change or the customer agrees, so a 2027 price rise normally applies only to new quotes and to ongoing work once your terms allow it. A ready-to-edit notice is below.

Rules checked on the ACCC, WA Small Business Development Corporation, WA Consumer Protection and NT Consumer Affairs websites on 12 November 2026.

General information only, not legal advice. For your situation, talk to a lawyer, your state or territory fair trading office, or your industry association.

Price rise notice template

Copy this into an email, a text or a letter and fill in the brackets. Delete any line that doesn't apply. Sole trader? Swap "we" and "our" for "I" and "my".

Price update from [Business name]

Hi [first name],

From [day and date, e.g. Monday 1 February 2027], our prices are changing. [One true sentence on why, e.g. "Our material and insurance costs have gone up this year."]

What this means for you:

  • Our call-out fee goes from $[old] to $[new], and our hourly rate from $[old] to $[new]. [List each price that changes, including GST if you charge it.]
  • Any quote you've already accepted stays at the quoted price.
  • Quotes we send from [date] use the new prices.
  • [For regular or ongoing work: "Your [service] on [schedule] moves to $[new] from [date]. If you'd rather not continue at the new price, just let us know before then."]

If you've got a question, call or text us on [number] or reply to this email.

Thanks for your business this year.
[Your name], [Business name], ABN [number]

Why each line is there:

  • What, when and why: the WA Small Business Development Corporation (SBDC) advises telling customers what's changing, when the new prices take effect and why, and giving as much notice as you can (SBDC).
  • The customer's name, not "Dear Customer": also the SBDC's advice, along with a direct way to reach you with questions (SBDC).
  • A true reason: anything you say about your services must be accurate, truthful and based on reasonable grounds (ACCC). If the reason is that your costs went up, say that. Don't blame a cost that didn't change.
  • Accepted quotes stay put: see the next section.
  • Full prices: when you show a price, show the total as a single figure, including taxes and any unavoidable fees (ACCC).

Quotes already accepted

A quote is usually legally binding once the customer accepts it. WA Consumer Protection says a quote forms a binding contract when accepted, and that if a bill comes in higher than the quote and you can't agree, the customer can refuse to pay the extra (WA Consumer Protection). NT Consumer Affairs says the customer can't be made to pay more than the quoted price unless there are special circumstances written into the quote or they agree to vary it, and that neither side can withdraw from or vary the contract without the other's consent (NT Consumer Affairs, Version 3, 2 January 2024). The same fact sheet says a quote should be in writing, detail the work and materials, and say how long it's valid.

So in practice:

  • Finish accepted jobs at the quoted price.
  • Put a "valid until" date on every quote you send from now on, so a quote from November 2026 isn't still open in March 2027.
  • If the customer asks for extra work, write it up as a variation and get it agreed before you do it.

Ongoing work and contracts

If you do regular work (a monthly service, a maintenance agreement, a cleaning schedule) on your own pre-written terms, those terms are likely a standard form contract. The ACCC presumes a contract is standard form, and the business that prepared it has to prove it isn't (ACCC).

  • Unfair terms are banned. Since 9 November 2023, proposing, using or relying on an unfair term in a standard form contract is banned and penalties apply (ACCC). A term a court finds unfair is void (ACCC).
  • The ACCC's price example: a term letting a business change the price at any time, where the customer can't end the contract, is likely unfair. A similar term that lets customers who don't agree end the contract without penalty is likely not (ACCC).
  • Small business customers are covered too if they have fewer than 100 employees or under $10 million annual turnover (ACCC).

That's why the template offers ongoing customers a way out before the new price starts. Check your own terms for any notice period you've already promised, and follow it.

Sending it by email or text

If the message also promotes something (a new service, a booking offer), treat it as a commercial message under the Spam Act: identify your business, include correct contact details and give an easy way to unsubscribe (ACMA). Send it to customers you already work with, not to a bought list.

Update everything on the same day

The SBDC suggests publishing the new prices on your website and updating signs and marketing material before they take effect (SBDC). For a tradie that usually means your price list, your quote template, your website and any Google Business Profile service prices. Old prices left on your site can mislead customers about what they'll pay.

Frequently asked questions

Can I put my price up on a quote the customer already accepted?

Usually not on your own. Once a customer accepts a quote it's usually a binding contract: WA Consumer Protection says so, and NT Consumer Affairs says neither side can withdraw from or vary it without the other's consent. The exception is a price-change term written into the quote itself. Otherwise, agree any change with the customer, in writing.

How much notice do I have to give for a price increase?

Check your own terms and any contract you've signed first, because a notice period you promised there is the one to follow. Beyond that, the WA Small Business Development Corporation's advice is to give customers as much notice as you can and tell them what's changing, why and when.

Can my terms say I can change prices at any time?

Be careful. In a standard form contract, an ACCC example says a term letting a business change the price at any time, with no way for the customer to end the contract, is likely to be unfair. A similar term that lets customers who don't agree leave without penalty is likely not. Unfair contract terms have been banned, with penalties, since 9 November 2023.

Do I have to give a reason for the price rise?

The ACCC and state guidance we checked doesn't require one for everyday trade work, but your own terms or contract might, and some industries (home building contracts, for example) have their own price-change rules. If you do give a reason, it has to be true: the ACCC says any claim a business makes about its products or services must be accurate, truthful and based on reasonable grounds.
