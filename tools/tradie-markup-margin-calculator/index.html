Markup and margin: same profit, different percentages

Markup and margin both describe the profit on a job. The difference is what you divide the profit by.

  • Markup is profit as a percentage of your cost. A job costs you $1,000 and you charge $1,250: the profit is $250, and $250 ÷ $1,000 is a 25% markup.
  • Margin is profit as a percentage of the price. Same job: $250 ÷ $1,250 is a 20% margin.

Same dollars, two different percentages. On any job that makes a profit, the markup figure is always bigger than the margin figure.

Why mixing them up underprices jobs

Say you've decided you want a 25% margin, so a quarter of everything the customer pays is profit. Then, quoting in a hurry, you add 25% to your costs.

The job costs you $8,000 all up. Add 25% and you quote $10,000. Your profit is $2,000, which is a 20% margin, not 25%.

To actually get a 25% margin, the price has to be $8,000 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = $10,666.67. That's $666.67 more profit on the same job.

It isn't a rounding error. It happens on every job priced that way, and the gap gets wider as the percentage goes up. At 50%, adding 50% to your costs gives you a 33.33% margin.

The formulas

  • Price from markup: price = cost × (1 + markup)
  • Price from margin: price = cost ÷ (1 − margin)
  • Markup to margin: margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup)
  • Margin to markup: markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin)

Margin can never reach 100%, because that would mean the job cost you nothing. Markup has no ceiling: a 100% markup doubles your cost, which is a 50% margin.

Get your costs right first

A percentage is only as good as the cost it's applied to. Before you worry about markup or margin, check the cost figure covers the whole job:

  • Materials at what they cost you. If you're registered for GST you can usually claim the GST back, so use ex-GST costs. If you're not registered, the GST you paid is part of your cost.
  • Labour: hours on the job times what an hour costs you, including super and other on-costs, for everyone working on it.
  • Other job costs such as travel, tip fees, equipment hire and permits.

Costs that aren't tied to one job, like insurance, your vehicle, phone and software, still need paying. Either build them into your hourly figure or make sure your markup covers them as well as your profit.

When to add GST

Only add GST if you're registered for it. If you are, work out your price ex GST first (that's where markup and margin apply), then add 10%. The GST you collect goes to the ATO, so it isn't profit and shouldn't count when you check your margin.

If you're not registered, don't add GST to your quotes or invoices.

As a general rule, the ATO says you must register for GST within 21 days once your GST turnover (gross business income, not counting GST) reaches $75,000 or more, or when you expect it to, for example in your first year of a new business. Below that, registering is optional, unless you want to claim fuel tax credits, which needs GST registration. Check the details for your situation on the ATO's Registering for GST page.

Common questions

Is a 25% markup the same as a 25% margin?

No. A 25% markup on a $1,000 cost gives a $1,250 price and $250 profit, which is a 20% margin. To get a 25% margin you need a 33.33% markup, a price of $1,333.33.

How do I convert markup to margin?

Margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup). For a 40% markup: 0.40 ÷ 1.40 = 28.57% margin.

Going the other way, markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin). For a 20% margin: 0.20 ÷ 0.80 = 25% markup.

Do I add GST before or after my markup?

After. Work out your price ex GST from your costs and markup (or margin), then add 10% GST on top, but only if you're registered for GST. The GST goes to the ATO, so leave it out when you check your profit and margin.

Should I enter my charge-out rate or what an hour costs me?

What an hour costs you (wages, super and other on-costs) if you want the markup to be your profit on labour. If you enter your charge-out rate instead, any profit already built into that rate is on top of what the calculator shows as profit.
