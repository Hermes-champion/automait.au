How the calculator works

  • Taxable income = business profit + wages
  • Income tax = 2026–27 resident rates on taxable income, less the low income tax offset (never below $0)
  • Medicare levy = 2% of taxable income
  • Set aside = income tax + Medicare levy − tax covered by your wages
  • Per week = set aside ÷ 52; per fortnight ÷ 26; per month ÷ 12

ExampleExample amounts only.

  • $60,000 profit, no other income. Income tax is $4,020 + 30% of $15,000 = $8,520.00. The low income tax offset is $325 − 1.5% of $15,000 = $100.00, so income tax is $8,420.00. Add the Medicare levy of $1,200.00 (2% of $60,000) for a total of $9,620.00. That's about 16.1% of profit: $185.00 a week or $801.67 a month.
  • $50,000 wages plus $20,000 profit. Tax on $70,000 is $12,920.00 including Medicare levy. Tax on the $50,000 of wages alone is $6,270.00, which your employer's withholding is assumed to cover. The extra $6,650.00 is what to set aside: about 33.3% of the $20,000 profit, because it's taxed at 30% plus the 2% levy and it reduces the low income tax offset.

Medicare levy

The ATO says the Medicare levy is 2% of your taxable income, paid on top of income tax. People on low incomes may pay a reduced levy or none. For 2025–26, the ATO's thresholds for a single person were $28,011 and $35,013. We couldn't find 2026–27 thresholds on that page when we checked, so this calculator always uses the full 2%. The Medicare levy surcharge is a separate charge and isn't included.

Low income tax offset

The ATO's low income tax offset page says residents with taxable income of $37,500 or less get $700 off their tax. Between $37,501 and $45,000 it's $700 minus 5c for every $1 over $37,500, and between $45,001 and $66,667 it's $325 minus 1.5c for every $1 over $45,000. It can reduce your tax to $0 but isn't paid out as a refund. The ATO works it out when you lodge. The calculator applies it automatically, to income tax only, not the Medicare levy.

Why set money aside?

When you earn wages, your employer withholds tax from each pay. Business income usually has no tax taken out, so the tax on your profit is due after you lodge your return. The ATO lists income as a sole trader, and not paying enough in PAYG instalments, among the reasons you may get a tax bill.

Once you've lodged a return with business income, the ATO may put you into PAYG instalments, which are quarterly prepayments of your tax. For individuals it's automatic when your latest return shows instalment income of $4,000 or more, tax payable of $1,000 or more, and estimated tax of $500 or more. New businesses can choose to start them. The ATO's own example divides the yearly instalment amount by 52 to work out what to put aside each week, which is how the weekly figure above is worked out.

A separate bank account for tax money can help keep it out of everyday spending. The ATO suggests this in its managing payments guide.

What this estimate leaves out

  • HELP, VSL and other study and training loan repayments.
  • The Medicare levy surcharge (for some people without private hospital cover), and Medicare levy reductions and exemptions.
  • Tax offsets other than the low income tax offset, such as the seniors and pensioners tax offset.
  • Super: personal super contributions you claim as a deduction, and any super you choose to put aside for yourself.
  • Capital gains, investment income, and anyone who isn't an Australian resident for the full year.
  • GST. If you're registered, keep the GST you collect separate. It isn't part of your profit.

Your actual tax is worked out when you lodge your return. If your situation is more complex, a registered tax agent can work it out properly.

Common questions

How much tax should I set aside as a sole trader?

It depends on your profit, because tax is worked out on your whole taxable income at the individual rates. Using this calculator's 2026–27 estimate, a sole trader with $60,000 profit and no other income would set aside about $9,620 for income tax and the Medicare levy, which is about 16.1% of profit or $185.00 a week.

If you also earn wages, the tax on your business profit is usually at a higher rate, because your wages use up the lower brackets first. Enter your own figures above for an estimate.

Do sole traders pay a different tax rate?

No. The ATO says a sole trader pays tax on all their income, including business income, based on their individual tax rate. You report business income and expenses in your individual tax return; there's no separate business tax return for sole traders.

What are the 2026–27 tax rates for Australian residents?

Per the ATO's resident tax rates for 2026–27: nil up to $18,200; 15c for each $1 over $18,200; $4,020 plus 30c for each $1 over $45,000; $31,020 plus 37c for each $1 over $135,000; and $51,370 plus 45c for each $1 over $190,000. These don't include the 2% Medicare levy.

The second rate is 15% for 2026–27, down from 16% in 2025–26.

Do I have to pay PAYG instalments?

Not always. The ATO says an individual, including a sole trader, is automatically entered into PAYG instalments if they have instalment income of $4,000 or more in their latest tax return, tax payable of $1,000 or more on their latest notice of assessment, and estimated (notional) tax of $500 or more. You can also choose to enter. Instalments you pay during the year are offset against your tax when you lodge your return.

Should I include GST in my profit?

No. Enter your profit without GST. If you're registered for GST, the GST you collect is paid to the ATO with your BAS, so keep it aside separately. The ATO suggests a separate bank account for your tax could help. The GST calculator works out the GST in a price.
